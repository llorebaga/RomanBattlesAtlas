01

EVENT OVERVIEW

The only time before the fifth century CE that Rome fell to a foreign enemy. It cost Rome its records, gave it a lasting dread of Gauls, and — because the recovery took barely a decade — became the event Romans cited to prove that Rome could not be finished by one defeat.

A Gallic war band destroyed a Roman army eleven miles from the city and then took Rome itself, holding everything but the Capitol until it was bought off.

The Senones had settled the Adriatic coast a generation earlier and moved inland against Clusium, which appealed to Rome. Roman envoys, on the tradition, joined the fighting instead of mediating, and the Gauls came south. The Romans met them in the open with an army the sources say was hastily raised and badly deployed, with the weight on a wing that could be turned. Everything after the battle is remembered in vignettes — the geese on the Capitol, the senators awaiting death in their doorways, the sword thrown onto the scales — which is what happens when the records themselves have burned.

THE BATTLE AREA

The Allia stream, about eleven miles north of Rome

The marker below is a representative location. Surviving sources rarely allow an exact battle line to be plotted.

Geographic uncertainty: approximately 16 km. The Allia is usually identified with a small tributary on the Tiber's left bank, but the identification and the bank are both argued.

EVIDENCE REGISTER

What remains uncertain

  • Roman and Greek chronologies for the sack differ by three or four years.
  • How much of the city was actually destroyed is disputed; the archaeological destruction layer is thinner than the literary account implies.
  • Whether the Capitol truly held out, or the story exists to soften the defeat, has been questioned since antiquity.

This page separates surviving testimony from modern reconstruction. It does not invent precise tracks, dialogue, weather, or unit positions.

FURTHER READING

Sources

Ancient testimony

  1. Livy, Ab Urbe Condita, Books 1–5The foundation to the Gallic sack. The fullest narrative that survives and the least reliable part of Livy: he is working from annalists who themselves had little to work from, and he says so.
  2. Polybius, Histories, Book 2Preserves the Gallic wars in Italy and the texts of the early treaties between Rome and Carthage.
  3. Plutarch, Life of CamillusThe Veientine war and the Gallic sack, in a life built to make Camillus a second founder of Rome.

Modern studies

  1. T. J. Cornell, The Beginnings of Rome (1995)The standard case for taking a controlled amount of the early tradition seriously.
  2. Gary Forsythe, A Critical History of Early Rome (2005)The sceptical counterweight: much of what Livy reports for the fifth century is reconstruction rather than record.
