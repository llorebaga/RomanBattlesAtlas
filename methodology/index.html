Methodology

History without false certainty

Ancient evidence is incomplete. Rather than smoothing that over, the atlas labels every route, coordinate, and force estimate with how well it is supported — so you always know whether you are looking at a fact or a reconstruction.

The five grades

  • Attested

    Strong primary or archaeological support.

  • Probable

    A widely accepted reconstruction, supported by several sources.

  • Disputed

    Credible interpretations disagree.

  • Speculative

    Drawn so a leg of the story can be followed, and labelled as such.

  • Traditional

    What the Roman annalists recorded, reported as memory rather than as evidence. Used for the early Republic, where the surviving accounts were written four centuries after the events.

Early Rome, and why it is graded differently

For the Punic and Macedonian wars there is Polybius, writing within living memory of some of it and with access to people who were there. For the early Republic there is nothing of the kind. Livy and Dionysius of Halicarnassus were writing in the age of Augustus about events four and five centuries earlier, from annalistic material that had already passed through the hands of families with reputations to protect and of Greek historians with literary models to satisfy. Rome’s own records had been thin to begin with, and the tradition itself held that much of what existed was lost when the Gauls burned the city.

So the atlas maps the Republic from 509 BCE, and marks that material traditional rather than fitting it into the four grades of evidence. The distinction matters: speculative says the atlas has reconstructed something to make a real sequence followable, while traditional says this is what Rome remembered about itself. Battles from these centuries keep their traditional dates because those dates are how the events are referred to, not because the year is secure — several are demonstrably duplicated within the annalistic record, and the sceptical view that a good deal of the fifth century is reconstruction rather than history is a serious one.

The regal period before 509 is not mapped at all. The wars of Romulus and his successors are foundation myth, and drawing them would put the first invented thing on the map.

What the atlas does not do

  • It does not invent dialogue, weather, precise formations, or undocumented daily movements.
  • It does not present a battle coordinate as a surveyed position. Markers are representative areas, with an uncertainty radius.
  • It does not draw a march it cannot justify: route waypoints are campaign stages, and each carries its own certainty and sources.
  • It does not show ancient frontiers as surveyed lines. Territory zones are schematic areas of control, clipped to the coastline.
  • It does not treat literary numbers as audited records. Force and casualty figures are given as ranges, attributed, and labelled.

Frontiers and territory

Where a frontier is known to have followed a real feature, the map follows that feature: the Ebro for the treaty limit in Iberia, the Apennine watershed for Rome’s northern edge, the Alpine crest between the Gallic peoples. Land held by independent peoples is left uncoloured on purpose — the Celtiberian interior, Liguria, Gaul beyond the Loire — because colouring it would be the error.

Sources

Primary narratives are kept separate from modern scholarship throughout the atlas.

Ancient testimony

  • Livy, Ab Urbe Condita, Books 1–5 — The foundation to the Gallic sack. The fullest narrative that survives and the least reliable part of Livy: he is working from annalists who themselves had little to work from, and he says so.
  • Livy, Ab Urbe Condita, Books 6–10 — 389 to 293 BCE, covering the Latin and Samnite wars. Better founded than the first pentad, but the battle narratives are still built on the assumption that early Rome fought the way late Rome did.
  • Livy, Periochae (the ancient summaries of the lost books) — Books 11–20 covered 292–219 BCE and are lost; what survives is a paragraph-long summary of each. It fixes years, consuls and outcomes and almost nothing else — which is why the Pyrrhic War is reconstructed mainly from Plutarch.
  • Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Roman Antiquities — A Greek history of early Rome written at Rome under Augustus, independent of Livy in places though drawing on the same annalists. Books 1–10 survive complete, to 443 BCE; everything after that — including the Samnite and Pyrrhic wars — is fragments.
  • Plutarch, Life of Camillus — The Veientine war and the Gallic sack, in a life built to make Camillus a second founder of Rome.
  • Plutarch, Life of Pyrrhus — The fullest surviving account of the Pyrrhic War, drawing at second hand on Pyrrhus' own memoirs and on Hieronymus of Cardia. With Livy's books lost, this is the narrative the war rests on.
  • Appian, Roman History: The Samnite Wars (fragments) — Fragments covering the Samnite and Pyrrhic wars, including the embassy of Cineas and the negotiations Rome refused.
  • Polybius, Histories, Book 1 — Principal surviving narrative of the First Punic War and the Mercenary War that followed it; composed well after the events and not free from interpretive problems.
  • Diodorus Siculus, Library of History, Book 23 (fragments) — Fragments of an account of the First Punic War independent of Polybius, preserving different figures in places.
  • Zonaras, Epitome of Histories, Book 8 — A twelfth-century epitome of Cassius Dio's lost early books. It preserves material found nowhere else — the Pyrrhic War, Sulci — at the cost of being three removes from any eyewitness.
  • Polybius, Histories, Book 2 — The years between the wars: Barcid Iberia, the Illyrian wars, and Rome's Gallic war in the Po valley. It also pauses to recount the Gallic invasion and sack of Rome, which is why it is cited for 390 as well.
  • Polybius, Histories, Book 3 — The causes of the Second Punic War, the march to Italy, and the campaigns through Cannae — where the book ends. It opens with Hannibal's accession in 221.
  • Polybius, Histories, Book 8 (fragments) — Preserves the fullest technical account of the defence of Syracuse and Archimedes' engines.
  • Polybius, Histories, Book 9 (fragments) — The siege of Capua and Hannibal's march on Rome, with Polybius' own assessment of the strategy.
  • Polybius, Histories, Book 10 (fragments) — Scipio's capture of New Carthage and the battle at Baecula, including the appraisal of Scipio's character on which much of his reputation rests.
  • Polybius, Histories, Book 11 (fragments) — The Metaurus and the reversed array at Ilipa — the fullest surviving account of the manoeuvre.
  • Polybius, Histories, Book 14 (fragments) — The burning of the Carthaginian and Numidian camps and the battle of the Great Plains.
  • Polybius, Histories, Book 15 (fragments) — Zama and the peace terms, including the deployment of both armies.
  • Livy, Ab Urbe Condita, Books 21–30 (the third decade) — Detailed but later, rhetorically shaped, and consistently pro-Roman. Where it follows Polybius it is useful; where it does not, its figures are often inflated. Book 21 opens with the background from 221.
  • Appian, Roman History: The Hannibalic War & The Punic Wars — Later compilation preserving otherwise lost material, and prone to dramatic invention where it does not.
  • Polybius, Histories, Book 18 — Principal account of Cynoscephalae and the settlement with Macedon, including the comparison of legion and phalanx.
  • Livy, Ab Urbe Condita, Books 31–33 — The fullest surviving narrative of the Second Macedonian War, largely following Polybius but pro-Roman where it departs from him.
